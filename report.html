1. Origin

I walk past bilingual street names every day in Hong Kong, and as a translation student I kept wondering how they were made: why is Nathan Road 彌敦道, a sound, while Garden Road is 花園道, a meaning? I chose Topic #26 because street names are short, public and countable — a small corpus can actually answer the question, instead of only illustrating it.

2. Background

Hong Kong is a bilingual city, and its street names carry both colonial history and local place names: British officials and merchants (Nathan, Chater, Elgin), Chinese villages and hills (Tai Wai, Castle Peak), and newer estates with parallel Chinese and English names. When I checked data.gov.hk before choosing the topic, there was no ready-made bilingual street-name list to download — only a one-query-at-a-time look-up service and raw geographic data. Translation theory gave me the lens: Vinay and Darbelnet’s procedures (borrowing, calque, literal translation) and Venuti’s foreignization and domestication, which map onto keeping the sound of the source name versus adapting it to the reader.

3. Gap

Nobody had counted it. Street-name lists exist for finding an address, but no classified corpus showed which translation strategy Hong Kong actually uses most, whether the pattern changes by street type, or whether the same English name can be rendered in two different ways. Without those numbers, claims about Hong Kong street-name translation were guesses. This project fills that gap with a 500-entry coded corpus that anyone can search and check.

4. Questions

Three questions. (1) Which translation strategy dominates Hong Kong street names — transliteration, literal, free or hybrid? (2) Does the pattern change across street types (Main Road, Secondary Road, Track)? (3) Can one English name be rendered in two ways, and if so, why?

5. Method

Corpus source

Lands Department Road Centreline, retrieved 19 November 2026 from the CSDI geoportal / data.gov.hk via the ArcGIS REST FeatureServer (layer 0, RoadCentreLine). The source is a government geographic dataset, so every entry is traceable to a STREETCODE.

Coordinates and region

Each street's coordinates were fetched from the same FeatureServer (returnGeometry=true, WGS84). The representative point is the middle vertex of the street's longest Road Centreline segment. Region is assigned from the coordinates by a stated rule: Hong Kong Island is south of the harbour (latitude at or below 22.29, longitude 114.03-114.33); Kowloon is the peninsula north of the harbour (latitude 22.29-22.355, longitude 114.10-114.24); everything else - the New Territories, Lantau and the outlying islands - is New Territories. The rule is approximate: streets near the boundaries are assigned by the rule, not by their administrative district. Counts: Hong Kong Island 113, Kowloon 124, New Territories 263.

Population and sampling

39,960 road segments → 12,265 unique STREETCODEs → 4,568 streets with both a Chinese and an English name. 7,680 unnamed segments and 17 English-only streets were excluded when defining the population (7,697 rejected at that stage). From the 4,568, a fixed-seed random sample of 500 was drawn: Python random.Random(20261205).sample(population sorted by STREETCODE, 500). No entry was rejected at the draw stage, because the filters had already been applied. Anyone using the same seed and the same population list can reproduce the identical sample.

Codebook

Four categories, decided on the proper-name element only: Transliteration (音譯), Literal (直譯), Free (意譯), Hybrid (混合). Each category has a definition, a theory source (Vinay & Darbelnet; Venuti 1995), a decision rule, a positive and a negative example, and a boundary case. The full codebook is published on the Codebook page. It was pilot-tested on the first 30 entries of the sample (Transliteration 23, Literal 2, Free 0, Hybrid 5) and revised once (v0.1 → v0.2) before the full coding.

Coding

The 500 entries were draft-coded with a rule-based classifier (Cantonese Jyutping sound matching plus a gloss-based meaning check), then every low-confidence entry was reviewed by hand and the classifier was fixed where it made systematic mistakes. A random spot-check of 50 high-confidence entries was read by hand, and the 30 pilot entries were re-checked — all 30 still match the codebook pilot codes. The classifier script is kept in the project repository for reproducibility. The clean sample file was not changed; the coded version is a separate file.

Full re-check (20 November 2026)

On 20 November 2026 the author asked for a full re-check of all 500 entries from the start. It found 3 errors, all corrected: ST391 逸林里 KENSINGTON LANE, Transliteration → Free (a parallel estate name, not a sound match); ST164 大圍第十街 TENTH STREET TAI WAI, Transliteration → Hybrid (第十, rendered as Tenth, had been swallowed by the classifier’s sound window); ST113 漁安苑道 YUE ON COURT ROAD, Transliteration → Hybrid (苑 is rendered as Court). The other 27 medium-confidence entries passed every screen. New counts: Transliteration 374, Literal 41, Free 34, Hybrid 51. The full check report is kept in the project files.

Quality check (brief 0.3.2)

A further random sample of 50 entries was drawn from the 500 with seed 20261206 (corpus_qc50_seed20261206.csv). Each of the 50 must be checked by hand against the Road Centreline record and marked human_checked = yes. Status on 20 November 2026: not yet done (0 of 50). The number of errors found and corrected will be reported here when the check is complete. This is separate from the full re-check above, which covered all 500 entries once.

Bar chart: Entries by category

The bar chart answers the first question at a glance: transliteration towers over the other three categories, 374 entries against 41, 34 and 51. Three in four street names in the sample are sound-based. This is not a close result, and it is the opposite of what the author expected before coding — literal translation looked like the obvious default, but the data says otherwise.

Donut: Share by category

The donut gives the same result as shares: transliteration 74.8%, hybrid 10.2%, literal 8.2%, free 6.8%. Free translation is the smallest slice, and 12 of its 34 entries are the Fairview Park (錦綉花園) streets — one estate accounts for more than a third of the whole category. Without that estate, free translation across Hong Kong is rarer still.

Heatmap: Category × street type

The heatmap answers the second question: transliteration leads in every street type — 29 of 40 Main Roads, 293 of 377 Secondary Roads, and 50 of 80 Tracks. But the pattern is not flat. Literal translation is almost entirely a Secondary Road phenomenon (38 of its 41 entries), while hybrid entries concentrate in Tracks (25 of 51, against only 16% of the sample being Tracks): village lanes typically combine a transliterated village name with a literally translated number or generic word, such as 莆上村第十五巷 Po Sheung Tsuen Lane No.15.

Treemap: category × street type

The treemap shows the same cross-tabulation as areas: one cell dominates the corpus — Transliteration × Secondary Road, 293 entries, 58.6% of the whole sample. The next largest cell, Transliteration × Track, has only 50. The treemap makes the concentration visible in a way the table does not: more than half of Hong Kong street-name translation, in this sample, is a single combination.

Word cloud: English words in street names

The word cloud’s largest words are ON (24), YUEN (24) and TAI (22), followed by WAN, TSUEN and TIN — all syllables of transliterated place names, not English meaning words. The cloud’s shape is itself the finding: street names are mostly proper names, so sound wins. FAIRVIEW (12) is the one exception that proves the pattern: it is the estate cluster behind the Free category, the same 12 Fairview Park streets, appearing as a single repeated English word.

One English name, two renderings

The third question is answered by Kensington. ST142 肯辛頓徑 KENSINGTON PATH is transliteration: the three syllables match sound by sound. ST391 逸林里 KENSINGTON LANE is free translation: 逸林 is the developer’s Chinese name for the One Hyde Park estate in Yuen Long, and Kensington is its parallel English name after the London district — neither is derived from the other, and the measured sound ratios are 0.33 and 0.0. The same English name is rendered twice, and the codebook decides by where the name came from, not by the letters on the sign.

Map: where the streets are

The map answers the same question geographically: the dots are spread across all three regions, and the colours show that transliteration is not a Central phenomenon - it is everywhere. The map's real use is the opposite direction: click a dot and the street's name, category and region appear, so any pattern in the tables can be checked against the actual place. The map is linked to the search and filters, like every other chart: it shows only the entries currently selected.

Historical factors: region and the name's origin

The third question - whether the pattern changes - can also be asked by region. The sample is unevenly spread (Hong Kong Island 113, Kowloon 124, New Territories 263), so the comparison must use shares, not raw counts. Against the sample baseline (New Territories 52.6%, Kowloon 24.8%, Hong Kong Island 22.6%), literal translation is over-represented on Hong Kong Island (36.6% of its 41 entries, against 22.6% of the sample), while free translation and hybrid are concentrated in the New Territories (70.6% each, against 52.6%). The two concentrations have different causes: the New Territories free and hybrid entries are mostly village and estate names - the 12 Fairview Park streets are free, and village lanes such as 莆上村第十五巷 are hybrid - while the Hong Kong Island literal entries are mostly older descriptive street names in Central, such as Garden Road and Hill Road. Transliteration itself is evenly spread (New Territories 50.5%, Kowloon 27.3%, Hong Kong Island 22.2%), close to the baseline in every region.

The same question at the level of a single generic word - 坊 - shows why the name's origin matters more than its location. Seven streets in the sample contain 坊, and its English rendering differs: FONG in 兆祥坊 and 秀華坊, SQUARE in 眾坊街, 元朗炮仗坊 and 安達坊, CIRCLE in 新亞坊, STEPS in 衛城坊. Both renderings occur on Hong Kong Island and in the New Territories, so geography alone does not explain the choice. What fits better is the naming event: 坊 is transliterated when it is part of a proper name (兆祥坊, 秀華坊), and translated when the English name describes the physical form - a square, a circle, steps. With only seven entries this is a suggestion, not a proof, but it is the pattern the data supports.

Lan Kwai Fong (蘭桂坊) is the clearest example of the second mechanism, although it is not in the sample and is not counted in the corpus. In the 19th century the lane was home to mui yan (媒人), traditional female matchmakers, and was known as Matchmaker Lane (媒人巷). The name 蘭桂坊 comes from the idiom 蘭桂騰芳 - orchid and cassia spreading fragrance - so the whole name, including 坊, is transliterated as Lan Kwai Fong: an idiom cannot be translated word by word, so the sound is kept. The street's later history (Disco Disco in 1978, Allan Zeman's California restaurant in 1983) made the name famous, but the transliteration was fixed by the naming event, not by the nightlife. The codebook's boundary case for Queensway works the same way: an established name is kept, and the category follows the name's origin.

Two cautions belong here. First, the region rule is approximate (see Method), so the regional shares are estimates, not administrative facts. Second, the 坊 case rests on seven entries; a larger sample of generic words (里, 巷, 徑) would be needed to test the naming-event explanation properly. Both points are why the region field and the coordinates are published in the corpus data: anyone can re-run the comparison.

7. Conclusion

I built a traceable 500-entry corpus from a government source, coded it with a four-category codebook, re-checked all 500 entries by hand in a full re-check, and published an explorer with six linked visualizations, including a map, that anyone can filter, sort and export. The answer to the first question is clear: Hong Kong street names are translated mainly by sound — 74.8% transliteration — because most street names are proper names. The pattern does vary by street type and by region, and the same English name can be rendered twice when a name is a parallel estate name rather than a transliteration. What is still missing is the random 50-entry quality check, the author’s own codebook definitions, and the reflection in §8.

10. AI declaration

Tools used

Muse (Muse Spark 1.3) was used for: retrieving the Road Centreline data, drawing the fixed-seed sample, and fetching the coordinates of all 500 streets; drafting the codebook (v0.1–v0.2); draft-coding the 500 entries with a rule-based classifier and hand-reviewing the low-confidence entries; building this website; and drafting the text of this report, except for §8. AI did not invent any data: every street name comes from the Lands Department Road Centreline.

What the author did and checked by hand

The author chose the topic, directed the full re-check of all 500 entries, decided the borderline codes, and will hand-check the 50 QC entries, rewrite the four codebook definitions in his own words, write the reflection in §8, and explain each category in the oral presentation.

Example prompts that worked well

“你最推薦哪個topic，and why?” — this produced a ranked shortlist with reasons, and the author chose #26 Hong Kong street-name translation from it. “敢你用多少少時間幫我由頭check500 條” — this led to a full re-check of all 500 entries from the start, which found 3 errors the classifier had missed.

One thing AI got wrong

The classifier coded ST164 大圍第十街 TENTH STREET TAI WAI as Transliteration with high confidence, because its sound window matched 大圍 (Tai Wai) and swallowed 第十 — but the English renders 第十 as Tenth, a literal element, so the entry is Hybrid. It was noticed only because the author asked for a full re-check of all 500 entries from the start, with a screen for dropped meaning words. A second weakness of the same kind: the classifier wrote “sound: 逸,林” for ST391 逸林里 although no sound was ever measured (ratios 0.33 and 0.0); it had assumed an unmatched Chinese chunk must be a phonetic rendering. Both errors are recorded in the full-check report, and both are why the brief requires human_checked.
